The AI Layoff Trap Is a Choice, Not a Forecast

The paper identifies a real risk. But its math does not prove that labor replacement will become the default use of AI.

August 17, 2026 · by Jordan Rothstein · ≈ 12 min read

☊ The Radio Edition narrated by a neural voice · 13:40

I saw a paper called The AI Layoff Trap making the rounds on X and decided to spend some time going through it.

I expected a fairly standard AI-doomer argument. That is not quite what it is.

The paper is smart, the mechanism is worth understanding, and the math gives the argument a level of precision that most AI labor discussions do not have. But I also think the headline travels much further than the model actually earns.

The paper proves a conditional statement.

If AI adoption primarily means replacing workers, if displaced income is not recovered through new work or other sources, and if the capacity created by AI does not generate enough new demand, then companies can collectively automate themselves into a weaker economy.

I agree that this can happen.

What I do not agree with is treating that outcome as the natural destination of AI adoption.

Math can make an argument more precise. It cannot make a chosen assumption inevitable.

What the paper gets right

The central mechanism is pretty simple.

When a company replaces a worker with AI, the company captures the full payroll savings. That benefit is immediate, visible, and easy to measure.

But the worker who loses that income also spends less money. The damage does not fall entirely on the company that made the layoff. It gets spread across restaurants, retailers, software companies, banks, insurers, landlords, and every other business competing for that person’s income.

The company keeps all of the savings while absorbing only a small portion of the demand loss.

That creates an externality. Every company has an incentive to keep automating because slowing down alone would leave it with a higher cost structure than its competitors. Even if executives understand the collective risk, awareness is not necessarily enough to change the individual decision.

Every company can make the “right” decision for itself while all of them together weaken the same market they depend on.

This is the strongest part of the paper. It moves the discussion beyond the basic observation that workers are also consumers. It explains why companies may keep cutting even when they can see where widespread cutting eventually leads.

In the authors’ model, each company captures the full labor-cost savings from automation while bearing only a fraction of the demand loss it creates. That causes competing firms to automate beyond the level that would collectively maximize their profits (Falk and Tsoukalas, 2026).

I think that is a real risk.

But it is still only one possible path.

Where the assumptions start doing the work

In the paper’s baseline model, every automated task displaces one worker. The automation rate and the layoff rate are treated as the same number.

Output is also fixed: a task performed by AI produces exactly the same amount as a task performed by a person. The authors explicitly state that this choice shuts down productivity and quality improvements so they can isolate the spending consequences of displacement.

The baseline also uses symmetric firms in one sector, holds wages fixed, and assumes business owners spend none of their additional income inside the modeled sector. Some of these assumptions are relaxed later, but they still shape the initial result and the way the paper is being discussed online (Falk and Tsoukalas, 2026).

None of that makes the model invalid.

Models are supposed to simplify reality so one mechanism can be studied clearly. The problem begins when the result of that narrow model gets repeated as though it is a forecast of the entire AI economy.

AI is not only a cheaper way to perform a fixed list of existing tasks.

It can increase the amount of work a team completes. It can improve the quality of work. It can compress learning curves, lower prices, make smaller customers profitable to serve, and allow companies to offer services that were previously too expensive to provide at all.

It can also create new tasks that did not exist before.

The paper does later introduce higher AI productivity. But even in that extension, the upside is modeled as more units of output from an automated task rather than the creation of entirely new products, services, occupations, or sources of demand.

There is an even more important detail inside that extension.

The planner’s objective is based on nominal income and spending, not the amount of real output consumers receive. When AI productivity rises, the model allows output to increase while prices fall proportionally. Because total nominal spending does not rise, the paper’s efficient automation benchmark remains unchanged.

The authors acknowledge this limitation directly. They note that their welfare measure understates the real consumption benefits of lower prices and clarify that their result is not a claim that better AI reduces total welfare (Falk and Tsoukalas, 2026).

That qualification matters.

The paper models a strategic distortion created by productivity. It does not establish that the productivity itself makes society poorer.

The trap disappears inside the paper’s own model

The most important part of the paper may be its treatment of income reabsorption.

The authors use a variable to represent how much of a displaced worker’s income is eventually replaced through reemployment, transfers, or other sources.

When displaced income is not fully replaced, the model produces over-automation.

When the income is fully replaced, the over-automation gap disappears.

When displaced workers move into jobs that pay more than the positions they lost, the model actually reverses. Instead of over-automation, it produces under-automation (Falk and Tsoukalas, 2026).

That changes the argument quite a bit.

The deciding variable is not AI capability by itself. It is how quickly lost income is recreated somewhere else in the economy.

How quickly do displaced people find new work?

What does that work pay?

Do falling costs create lower prices and more demand?

Do businesses use their new capacity to enter additional markets?

Do new products and companies appear quickly enough to absorb workers?

Does the value created by AI remain concentrated, or does it return to the economy through wages, ownership, investment, and consumption?

I think of this as reabsorption velocity.

A transition where productive work and income are recreated in six months looks very different from one where the adjustment takes fifteen years. Both economies may eventually “recover”, but that does not make the experiences of the displaced workers remotely equivalent.

The paper assumes that incomplete reabsorption is the relevant case. That is a valid risk to study, but it is still an assumption about the path the economy takes.

It is not an unavoidable property of AI.

What the evidence says so far

We do not currently have proof that an economy-wide AI layoff trap is happening.

We also do not have enough evidence to confidently say that it will not happen.

AI adoption is still early, and companies may reduce labor demand through slower hiring or unfilled positions long before those changes appear as formal AI layoffs.

But the evidence we do have does not look like a clean one-for-one replacement story.

A 2026 U.S. Census Bureau study using nationally representative business data found that 66% of firms using AI reported using it solely to augment tasks. Only 2% reported an AI-related decrease in employment. Adoption was also still fairly narrow inside many businesses, with most users deploying AI across only a small number of functions and tasks (Bonney et al., 2026).

That does not prove augmentation will remain the dominant model. The data is early, and some of it depends on how companies describe their own decisions.

But it does challenge the idea that worker replacement is already the default outcome.

We also have real evidence that AI can supercharge the workers who remain.

A published study of 5,172 customer-support agents found that access to an AI assistant increased productivity by 15% on average. Less experienced and lower-skilled workers saw an approximately 30% increase in issues resolved per hour. Workers with two months of experience and AI assistance performed about as well as workers with more than six months of experience who did not have the tool.

The system did not simply perform the job in place of the workers. It helped spread some of the knowledge and practices of stronger employees across the rest of the team (Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond, 2025).

That is a very different economic mechanism from the one-for-one swap.

AI can reduce the amount of labor needed for a given level of output. But it can also make a less experienced worker productive faster, help a smaller team attempt more difficult work, or allow a business to handle demand that it previously could not serve.

There are warning signs as well.

A separate 2026 Census working paper found that employment among workers ages 22 to 24 declined by 12% over ten quarters in the industry-state groups most exposed to AI. The author also identified earlier trend changes around the pandemic that complicate a perfectly clean causal story, but the decline in early-career hiring is still important (Tucker, 2026).

This may be where the displacement risk becomes visible first.

Not necessarily through one enormous wave of layoffs, but through smaller entry-level classes, fewer junior positions, and roles that quietly disappear when someone leaves.

There is a strange tension here.

AI may dramatically improve the performance of the junior workers who do get hired while reducing the number of junior workers a company believes it needs to hire.

AI may dramatically improve the performance of the junior workers who do get hired while reducing the number of junior workers a company believes it needs to hire.

That matters because entry-level work is not only inexpensive labor. It is how people learn an industry, build judgment, make mistakes under supervision, and eventually become the experienced workers companies depend on.

A company can remove the bottom rung of the ladder and continue operating for years because it already has senior people.

The problem only becomes obvious later, when it realizes it stopped creating the next generation of them.

AI creates a capacity dividend

The binary argument around AI is usually framed as replacement versus augmentation.

I think there is a more useful way to look at it.

Every successful AI deployment releases capacity.

It may release employee time, operating cost, management attention, technical expertise, or the ability to process a much larger volume of work.

I have started thinking about this as the AI capacity dividend.

That dividend can be used in several ways.

A company can keep output fixed and reduce headcount.

It can maintain the team and increase output.

It can lower prices and reach more customers.

It can improve service, create a new product, or start performing work that never made financial sense before.

It can also do some combination of all of these.

The technology creates the capacity. Management decides where the dividend goes.

I see this directly in insurance automation.

One insurance agency can use AI to process its existing quote volume with fewer people. That is a completely valid business case, and in some companies it will be the chosen path.

Another agency can use the same technology to approach more carriers for every customer, remarket more renewals, respond to leads in minutes instead of hours, and profitably serve smaller accounts that previously did not justify the manual effort.

Before the automation, that additional work was not being done at all.

After the automation, it becomes economical.

In that case, AI is not dividing the same amount of work among fewer people. It is increasing the total amount of work the market can support.

The technology is the same.

The management decision is not.

Most AI business cases currently begin with hours saved, labor removed, and headcount avoided. Those numbers are easy to measure and easy to place into a spreadsheet.

It is much harder to measure the customers we can now serve, the additional work we can now complete, or the products that become possible because the cost of intelligence and execution has fallen.

But that may be where the larger value sits.

A warning, not a forecast

The AI Layoff Trap identifies a legitimate failure mode.

If every boardroom defines AI ROI as “How much headcount can we remove?”, companies may collectively create the exact problem the authors describe.

Payroll falls. Income becomes more concentrated. Consumer demand weakens. Every company continues cutting because no individual company can afford to stop first.

That future is possible.

But the paper does not prove it is inevitable.

The authors themselves describe the work as identifying a structural vulnerability rather than diagnosing an active crisis. They acknowledge that the empirical signature would require displacement at a scale and speed beyond what had materialized by the paper’s June 2026 revision (Falk and Tsoukalas, 2026).

The model shows one path clearly.

It does not prove that path will win.

AI can be used to remove payroll from the existing economy. It can also lower the cost of creating products, delivering services, starting companies, and completing work that was previously uneconomical.

The distinction will not be decided by the technology alone.

It will be decided by what companies choose to optimize for.

So the AI ROI question should not only be:

How much labor can we remove?

It should also be:

What can we now afford to create?

That is not a reason to dismiss the layoff trap.

It is a reason not to confuse a warning with a forecast.

AI does not decide whether productivity becomes layoffs or growth.

Management does. ■
